New Patient Admission (Triage)

When this applies

A new patient is being triaged/admitted — you weren't following her before now.

Step 1: Chart review BEFORE you walk in (aim for 3-5 min)

What specifically to look for on the ultrasound report

  • Dating/EDD: was it confirmed by early US — does it match what's documented as her EDD now?
  • Anatomy survey: completed? Any noted anomalies?
  • Growth (EFW + percentile): EFW = Estimated Fetal Weight, calculated by the ultrasound from measurements of the head, abdomen, and femur. It's plotted on a growth chart as a percentile for gestational age — where does it sit, and, if multiple scans exist, is the percentile trend stable or crossing down (concerning for IUGR = Intrauterine Growth Restriction)?
  • Amniotic fluid: measured as AFI (Amniotic Fluid Index — sum of the deepest fluid pocket in all 4 quadrants of the uterus) or SDP (Single Deepest Pocket — just the single largest pocket found anywhere) — is it normal, oligohydramnios (too little fluid), or polyhydramnios (too much)?
  • Placental location: clear of the internal os? Any noted previa or low-lying placenta?
  • Presentation: vertex, breech, or transverse — and is this the most recent scan (presentation can change until late in pregnancy)?
  • Dopplers (if done — usually only if growth-restricted or other risk factor): a special ultrasound mode that measures blood flow resistance. Umbilical artery Dopplers assess how easily blood flows from baby to placenta (high resistance = placental insufficiency); MCA (Middle Cerebral Artery) Dopplers assess fetal brain blood flow, which can show a compensatory "brain-sparing" pattern in a stressed fetus. Are they normal or abnormal resistance?
If you walk in already knowing dating, growth trend, fluid, placental location, and presentation, you can focus the actual interview on the chief complaint instead of re-deriving the whole pregnancy history from scratch.

Step 2: Offer to take the history

  • Don't wait to be asked — ask the PGY3/resident "can I take the history on the new patient?"
  • Use what you already pulled from the chart as your starting point — confirm rather than re-ask

Step 3: Structure the interview

  1. Chief complaint in her own words
  2. HPI specific to the complaint (see the relevant scenario tab — Bleeding, ROM, PTL, Triage Complaints, etc. — for what to ask)
  3. Confirm/update what you pulled from the chart rather than re-asking all of it
  4. Pertinent ROS (headache/visual changes, RUQ pain, fever, fetal movement)

Step 4: Present with a differential + plan, not just facts

"This is [name], a [age]yo G_P____ at [EGA] by [dating method], presenting with [chief complaint]. [HPI]. Per her chart, growth has been [appropriate/trending down], fluid [normal], placenta [clear of os / previa noted], presentation [vertex/breech]. She denies [pertinent negatives]. Exam/monitor: [findings]. This is most consistent with [assessment], and my plan would be to [__]."
Notice this pulls directly from your chart review (growth, fluid, placenta, presentation) — that's what turns a bare fact-recitation into a presentation that shows you did the prep work.

Following a Laboring Patient

When this applies

You've been assigned a patient in labor and are checking in over the course of your shift.

What you should know about her specifically (not just the strip)

  • How was her due date determined — optimal early dating, or LMP only?
  • Gravidity/parity and prior pregnancy outcomes (prior delivery type, complications)
  • Current pregnancy complications and how they're being managed
  • Has her routine prenatal care been complete and on time?
  • If induced: why, and which induction/augmentation agents are being used (see Normal Labor tab)
  • Duration of latent vs. active labor for this specific patient so far

Ongoing monitoring

  1. Check the board periodically — still assigned?
  2. Pop in without hovering; ask the nurse "anything change?"
  3. Review her FHR tracing at least once a day, ideally with the resident — walk through it systematically (see "DR C BRAVADO" framework on the FHR/Fetal Status tab: risk factors → contractions → baseline → variability → accelerations → decelerations → overall category), form your own Category I/II/III read, then compare it to what the resident says
  4. Ask to accompany the next cervical exam (typically q4h in latent labor, q2h in active labor)
  5. Stay near the room as she approaches complete
Do not perform cervical checks yourself.

How to Weave Multiple Numbers Into One Presentation

"[Name] is now [X] hours into active labor, [dilation trend — e.g., progressed from 6 to 8cm over the last 2 hours]. The pattern: state the trend (not a snapshot) → state contraction adequacy with its actual definition → state the tracing category → connect the timing to the specific threshold, proactively, before being asked. Using 3+ of these numbers together in one coherent statement is what separates "knows some facts" from "has the framework."

Common mistakes

  • Checking in so rarely that you miss a meaningful change (or so often it becomes hovering)
  • Reading the tracing only when the resident does, instead of forming your own read first
  • Not knowing whether she's on Pitocin/at what rate when asked

Escalate when

Any new Category II/III finding, a fever, or a maternal symptom (severe pain, bleeding change) — tell the nurse/resident right away, don't wait for your next scheduled check-in.

Antepartum Coverage

When this applies

A patient is admitted but not in labor — being monitored for a medical/obstetric indication (e.g., preeclampsia, PTL, PPROM latency).

Step by step

  1. Shadow MFM (Maternal-Fetal Medicine) rounds on Mondays, after signout
  2. Review her chart before rounds — know the admitting indication and the specific monitoring plan (see the matching tab: HTN/Preeclampsia, Preterm Labor, or ROM/Chorio)
  3. Deliveries and C-sections always take priority over antepartum rounding — if one is happening, go to that instead

Delivery Imminent — You're Called In

When this applies

A nurse or resident tells you a delivery is close, or you notice your patient is approaching complete.

Step by step

  1. Go immediately — gown + gloves on even before the room/table is ready
  2. Position where directed (usually beside, not in front)
  3. Help with comfort/positioning: ice chips, heat pack, holding legs
  4. Gown on delivery table + pull your own gloves early — have them ready before the table is set up
  5. Watch for cues to help hand-over-hand with infant delivery and/or placenta — don't grab without being invited
  6. Help retract during any vaginal repairs afterward

Full clinical detail (shoulder dystocia algorithm, etc.) is on the Delivery Day / Dystocia tab.

  • Standing where you block the resident's/attending's view or access

Escalate when

Called to a C-Section

When this applies

You're told to scrub in (or asked if you'd like to) for a C-section.

Step by step

  1. Confirm you're actually scrubbing (vs. observing) before gowning
  2. Pull up the H&P/indication on your phone/workstation en route if possible
  3. Introduce yourself to the patient in pre-op/holding if you can catch her before the OR
  4. Scrub in properly; in the room, ask "where do you want me" rather than guessing
  5. Follow graded responsibility as offered: suture-cutting → retraction → skin suturing/knot-tying
  6. Limit talking — the patient is awake
Do not scrub into a STAT C-section unless instructed.

Overhead Emergency (STAT section, dystocia, PPH)

When this applies

An emergency is called or unfolding — either your patient or another one on the floor.

Step by step

  1. Move toward the room but stay out of the way unless waved in
  2. Don't ask questions during the event — debrief after
  3. If already in the room when it starts (e.g., your own patient), step to the wall unless given a task
  4. After it resolves, ask a resident to walk you through what happened — a genuine teaching opportunity
Never insert yourself into a code/emergency uninvited — you can be more harmful than helpful in a crowded room.

Don't Know What's Going On / Getting Pimped

When this applies

You've walked into a situation without context, or you're asked a question you don't know the answer to.

Step by step

  1. Ask the nurse first — fast, and they're usually happy to orient you
  2. Pull the chart yourself before asking a resident something Epic could answer
  3. When pimped: say what you know, then name the gap ("I know it's related to X but not sure of the exact criteria") — don't guess and state it as fact
  4. Look it up right after (this guide, UpToDate) and follow up later if relevant — shows initiative

Common mistakes

  • Guessing confidently instead of naming the gap — attendings notice and prefer honesty
  • Never circling back after looking something up, so the same gap resurfaces later

Escalate when

N/A — this is itself the resolution step for uncertainty; there's no further escalation needed beyond asking.

Induction / Augmentation Agents — Explained

Indications for Induction of Labor (IOL)

  • Maternal: preeclampsia/gestational HTN (see HTN tab — timing depends on severity/GA), chronic HTN, pregestational or poorly-controlled gestational diabetes, intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP — see Triage Complaints/Itching, typically induced ~36-39w depending on bile acid level), significant renal disease, certain cardiac conditions, antiphospholipid syndrome/other maternal coagulopathies, other maternal medical conditions where continuing pregnancy poses risk
  • Fetal: post-term or late-term pregnancy (typically offered ≥39w electively per ARRIVE trial, or medically indicated by 41-42w), IUGR/growth restriction, oligohydramnios, non-reassuring antenatal testing (abnormal NST/BPP/Dopplers), Rh alloimmunization/isoimmunization with fetal anemia risk, multiple gestation (timing varies by chorionicity — e.g., uncomplicated dichorionic twins ~38w, monochorionic ~34-37w), suspected fetal macrosomia (more often discussed than acted on alone — usually combined with another indication like diabetes)
  • Membrane/infection-related: PROM at or near term (see ROM/Chorio tab), chorioamnionitis (delivery is part of treatment regardless of GA)
  • Other: fetal demise, logistical/elective induction at ≥39w in an otherwise low-risk pregnancy (per ARRIVE — see Key Trials tab), maternal request in appropriate candidates, prior stillbirth (often prompts earlier delivery timing discussions)
  • Contraindications to induction (same general contraindications as labor itself): placenta/vasa previa, transverse lie, active genital herpes, prior classical C-section or extensive uterine surgery, umbilical cord prolapse, category III tracing (delivery indicated but not via induced labor)
On the board/in the details column, IOL is often paired with the reason (e.g., "IOL GDMA1," "IOL dFM & FGR") — that pairing IS the indication, so read the abbreviation next to IOL as the answer to "why is she being induced."
  • Cervical ripening — the cervix has to soften/thin/dilate somewhat before labor can be effectively induced (see Bishop Score). If it's unfavorable, you "ripen" it first with:
    • Foley balloon (mechanical): a catheter with a balloon is placed through the cervix and inflated — the balloon's pressure against the internal os physically stretches and dilates the cervix (no medication involved)
    • Misoprostol (PGE1): a prostaglandin pill placed vaginally or orally that softens/ripens the cervix and can also stimulate contractions
    • Dinoprostone (PGE2, brand name Cervidil): a prostaglandin inserted vaginally (like a small tampon-shaped insert) that ripens the cervix over several hours
  • Oxytocin (Pitocin): a synthetic version of the hormone that stimulates uterine contractions — given as a continuous IV drip that's titrated up gradually to build an effective contraction pattern; this is what most people mean by "being induced" or "augmented"
  • Amniotomy (AROM = Artificial Rupture Of Membranes): the provider uses a small hook-like tool to manually break the amniotic sac ("break the water") — this can jump-start or strengthen labor on its own, and is often done alongside Pitocin. Usually only done once the fetal head is engaged in the pelvis (so there's no space for the umbilical cord to slip down first — a cord prolapse risk)
    • Indications: to induce or augment labor (speeds up active phase and can reduce need for as much Pitocin); to allow internal monitoring (placing a fetal scalp electrode or IUPC when external monitoring is inadequate); to assess fluid (checking for meconium) when clinically relevant; sometimes done simply to move a stalled labor forward when the cervix is otherwise favorable
    • Contraindications/cautions: unengaged fetal head (cord prolapse risk), malpresentation, active genital herpes lesion, vasa previa or unknown placental location, HIV/Hep C with high viral load (can slightly increase transmission risk — case-by-case)
Tachysystole = too many contractions — specifically >5 contractions in a 10-minute window, averaged over 30 minutes. This matters because contractions that are too frequent don't give the placenta/fetus enough recovery time between them, which can cause fetal heart rate changes. First step: reduce/stop Pitocin, reposition the mother, consider terbutaline (a medication that relaxes the uterine muscle to quickly calm down an overly active contraction pattern).

Other Labor-Monitoring Terms Worth Knowing

  • Montevideo units (MVUs): a way of quantifying contraction strength using an internal pressure catheter (IUPC, below) — you sum the pressure generated by each contraction above baseline over a 10-minute window. >200 MVUs is generally considered an "adequate" contraction pattern for labor to progress.
  • IUPC (Intrauterine Pressure Catheter): a thin catheter placed inside the uterus (alongside the fetus, through the cervix) that directly measures contraction strength in real numbers — used when you need to confirm contractions are truly adequate (e.g., before calling a labor arrest) rather than just estimating by feel or the external monitor
  • Toco: shorthand for tocodynamometer — the external belt/sensor on the mother's abdomen that tracks contraction frequency and relative timing (not true strength, unlike an IUPC)

Fetal Heart Rate & Fetal Status

Read Every Strip — "DR C BRAVADO"

  • Determine Risk — check for maternal/fetal risk factors that raise your baseline suspicion before you even look at the strip:
    • Maternal: preeclampsia/HTN, diabetes (GDM or pregestational), fever/chorio, post-term or preterm gestation, prior C-section (uterine rupture risk), oligo/polyhydramnios, on Pitocin or Mag
    • Fetal: IUGR/growth restriction, known anomalies, abnormal Dopplers, multiple gestation, breech/malpresentation
    • Why it matters: the same tracing (e.g., minimal variability) is read with more concern in a growth-restricted fetus than in an otherwise low-risk one — risk factors set your threshold for concern
  • Review Contractions (freq, duration, strength)
  • BRA: Baseline rate (110–160 normal)
  • Variability (absent/minimal/moderate/marked)
  • Accelerations (present = reassuring)
  • Decelerations (early/late/variable/prolonged)
  • Overall assessment → Category I/II/III

Intrauterine Resuscitation — "LOOK"

  • Lateral positioning (off back)
  • Oxygen if hypoxia suspected
  • Off Pitocin — stop/reduce for tachysystole (too-frequent contractions) or Cat II/III tracing
  • Keep fluids going — IV bolus for hypotension (esp. post-epidural, since epidurals can drop maternal BP and reduce placental blood flow)
  • Also: check for cord prolapse/rapid cervical change; consider amnioinfusion (infusing sterile fluid into the uterus through a catheter to create a cushion around the umbilical cord, relieving compression) for variable decels; consider terbutaline (a medication that relaxes uterine muscle) for tachysystole

Placenta Previa

  • Pathophys: abnormal implantation over/near internal os (risk: prior C-section/uterine surgery, multiparity). Bleeding as the lower segment thins/cervix effaces disrupts placental vessels — painless because non-myometrial.
  • Dx: TVUS showing placental edge relative to internal os.
  • Mgmt: pelvic rest, no intercourse/digital exams; C-section at term (~36–37w) if persistent.

Abruption

  • Pathophys: premature separation of normally-implanted placenta, usually maternal vessel rupture (risk: HTN/preeclampsia, trauma, cocaine, prior abruption) → retroplacental hematoma → myometrial irritation = pain + hypertonic contractions; can be concealed.
  • Dx: clinical (pain + bleeding + tender rigid uterus + FHR changes); US has low sensitivity — normal US does NOT rule it out.
  • Mgmt: depends on severity/FHR — reassuring + preterm may allow expectant mgmt + steroids; nonreassuring or maternal instability → emergent delivery.

  3. Vitals (maternal hemodynamic status), fundal tone/tenderness on exam
  4. FHR category — assess before any pelvic exam
  5. Do not perform digital exam — get placental location from chart/US first

Hypertensive Disorders / Preeclampsia / HELLP

Severity Spectrum

  • Gestational HTN: BP ≥140/90 after 20w, no proteinuria/end-organ involvement
  • Preeclampsia: + proteinuria OR end-organ dysfunction
  • Preeclampsia with severe features: BP ≥160/110 (x2, ≥4h apart), headache unresponsive to Tylenol, visual changes, RUQ/epigastric pain, plt <100k, Cr >1.1 or 2x baseline, LFTs ≥2x normal, pulmonary edema
One severe feature alone is enough to call "severe" — proteinuria isn't required if a severe feature is present.

Pathophys

Abnormal placental spiral artery remodeling → placental ischemia → release of anti-angiogenic factors (sFlt-1, soluble endoglin) → systemic endothelial dysfunction → vasoconstriction, ↑vascular permeability, microthrombi. HELLP = microangiopathic hemolysis + hepatic microthrombi/ischemia + platelet consumption from endothelial injury — classically presents like "gastroenteritis" (n/v, RUQ pain).

Management

  • Mag sulfate — seizure prophylaxis (NMDA antagonism/cerebral vasodilation)
  • Antihypertensives if severe range (labetalol/hydralazine/nifedipine)
  • Definitive tx = delivery — severe features → deliver ≥34w; without severe features → can go to 37w
  • Eclamptic seizure → ABCs first, then Mag (NOT antiepileptics like phenytoin)
Mag toxicity: loss of DTRs → respiratory depression → cardiac arrest. Antidote = calcium gluconate.

Data to Collect & How to Present

  1. BP trend (not just one reading) — pull last several values
  2. Symptom screen: headache, visual changes, RUQ/epigastric pain
  3. Labs: CBC (platelets), LFTs, Cr, urine protein:Cr ratio
  4. DTRs/clonus if on Mag; I/Os if on Mag (renal clearance)
  5. Fetal status: FHR category, growth/fluid if IUGR concern
"[Name], G_P__ at [EGA], with [gestational HTN/preeclampsia], BP trending [__/__], [with/without severe-range readings]. She [endorses/denies] headache, visual changes, RUQ pain. Pimping Pearl

Why do we give Mag for preeclampsia and not an antiepileptic?
Mag is proven specifically for eclamptic seizure prophylaxis/treatment (MAGPIE trial); antiepileptics like phenytoin are not standard and are a classic wrong-answer trap.

Fetal fibronectin (fFN — a protein that leaks from the amniotic sac interface when the cervix is remodeling; tested 22–34w, membranes intact, minimal dilation) and TVUS cervical length (Trans-Vaginal UltraSound-measured cervical length) both have strong negative predictive value to rule OUT true preterm labor.

Multifactorial — subclinical infection/inflammation, uterine overdistension, cervical insufficiency, or vascular pathology (abruption) → final common pathway of prostaglandin release → cervical ripening + contractions before 37w.

Pimping Pearls

Why give betamethasone before 34 weeks but generally not after?
Benefit (reduced RDS/neonatal morbidity) is well established <34w; benefit still shown 34-36+6w in some patients (ALPS trial) but weighed against risk of neonatal hypoglycemia.
Why avoid indomethacin after 32 weeks?
Risk of premature closure of the fetal ductus arteriosus increases with advancing gestational age.

Shoulder Dystocia — Pathophys → Dx → Mgmt

Pathophys: anterior shoulder impacts maternal pubic symphysis after head delivers (risk: macrosomia/GDM, prior dystocia, prolonged 2nd stage, operative delivery). Bony obstruction, not power problem — more pushing/fundal pressure worsens impaction.

Dx: turtle sign (head retracts against perineum), failure of restitution, difficulty delivering anterior shoulder.

Mgmt — HELPERR:

  • Help — call for help
  • Evaluate for episiotomy
  • Legs — McRoberts maneuver: sharply flex the mother's hips up toward her chest (like pulling her knees to her shoulders) — this rotates the pelvis and flattens the curve of the lower spine, widening the effective pelvic outlet
  • Pressure — suprapubic pressure: an assistant pushes down/inward just above the mother's pubic bone to push the baby's shoulder out from behind it (different from fundal pressure at the top of the uterus, which is NOT used here)
  • Enter maneuvers — internal rotation (Woods screw / Rubin): the provider reaches in and manually rotates the baby's shoulders from the front-to-back (AP) diameter into an angled position, which is roomier — Woods screw rotates by pushing the posterior shoulder, Rubin by pushing the anterior shoulder
  • Remove the posterior arm — the provider reaches in, locates the baby's back (posterior) arm, and sweeps it out across the chest and delivers it first, which effectively narrows the shoulder width and frees the impacted shoulder
  • Roll the patient — Gaskin maneuver: repositioning the mother onto her hands and knees (all-fours) — gravity and the change in pelvic dimensions in this position can dislodge the impacted shoulder
Document time from head to body delivery.

Pimping Pearls

Why does fundal pressure make shoulder dystocia worse?
It's a bony impaction problem, not a power problem — more pushing force wedges the shoulder further against the symphysis.
Why does operative vaginal delivery require specific criteria?
Operative vaginal delivery = using forceps or a vacuum device to assist delivery of the baby's head during the second stage, instead of a spontaneous push-only delivery. Because of the risks involved (maternal laceration, fetal injury), it requires confirmed criteria first: full cervical dilation, ruptured membranes, known fetal station/head position, adequate pain control, an empty maternal bladder, and willingness to abandon the attempt and move to C-section if it isn't working.

Definition: ≥1000mL blood loss (any route) or signs/symptoms of hypovolemia, regardless of delivery mode.

Atony Management Ladder

  1. Bimanual uterine massage
  2. Oxytocin (first line)
  3. Methylergonovine — avoid in HTN
  4. Carboprost/Hemabate — avoid in asthma
  5. Misoprostol — another prostaglandin uterotonic, can be given rectally/orally/sublingually
  6. TXA (tranexamic acid): a medication that helps blood clot by blocking the breakdown of clots (antifibrinolytic) — doesn't make the uterus contract, but reduces bleeding as an adjunct
  7. Escalate if bleeding continues despite the above:
    • Uterine balloon tamponade (e.g., Bakri balloon) — a balloon device is placed inside the uterus and inflated, applying direct pressure to the bleeding uterine wall from the inside, like packing a wound
    • B-Lynch suture — a large suture placed around the outside of the uterus during surgery that compresses it like a tight brace, mechanically squeezing it closed
    • Uterine artery embolization — an interventional radiology procedure that blocks off the blood vessels feeding the uterus
    • Hysterectomy — surgical removal of the uterus; last resort when all else fails

Treat the specific "T" you find: repair lacerations, remove retained tissue, correct coagulopathy with blood products.

Pimping Pearls

Why is fibrinogen checked first in suspected obstetric coagulopathy?
Fibrinogen is the first factor to drop and drops disproportionately in obstetric hemorrhage/DIC (Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation — e.g., from abruption or AFE/Amniotic Fluid Embolism) compared to other pregnancies' baseline elevated fibrinogen.
Why is oxytocin first-line for atony over ergot/prostaglandin agents?
Best safety profile, fewest contraindications, and standard first-line per ACOG — other agents reserved for refractory cases or when oxytocin is contraindicated/insufficient.

Assessment Framework — How to Write/Say the "A"

The General Formula

[Age] G_P____ at [EGA] [+ key risk factor if relevant], [admitted for / presenting with __], now [current status / key finding], concerning for OR consistent with [diagnosis/problem]. [If uncertain: "differential includes X vs Y, favoring X given __."]
  • An assessment is a synthesis, not a diagnosis label alone — it should show your reasoning, not just name a disease
  • Formula: Identifier + relevant context → current key finding → your interpretation
  • If it's a normal/reassuring situation, say so explicitly — "reassuring," "appropriate," "uncomplicated" — don't just go silent on the assessment because nothing is wrong
  • One line is usually enough; add a differential only when the diagnosis is genuinely uncertain

How to Actually Derive the Assessment (not just phrase it)

The formula above tells you how to say the assessment. This is the checklist for how to arrive at it — the actual numbers to check, in order, before you decide what your interpretation is.

Worked example: "Normal laboring patient, progressing well"

  1. Confirm what phase she's in: Latent (<6cm) or Active (≥6cm)? This changes which clock you're using.
  2. Check the rate of cervical change, not just the current number:
    • Pull the last 2 exams and the time between them — e.g., "6cm → 8cm over 2 hours" = ~1cm/hr
    • Rough expected pace in active labor: multips often ~1-2cm/hr, nullips can be slower (~0.5-1.5cm/hr) — modern (Zhang) curves are slower than the old Friedman numbers, so don't panic at a nulliparous patient moving ~0.5cm/hr
    • The number that actually matters for calling something abnormal is the arrest threshold, not the rate: ≥6cm + ROM + no change at all for ≥4h with adequate contractions (or ≥6h if contractions are inadequate/being augmented) — see Normal Labor tab
  3. Check contraction adequacy (this gates whether "no progress" even counts as a real arrest): freq q2-3min by palpation, or Montevideo units >200 if an IUPC is in place. If contractions are NOT adequate, "no cervical change" doesn't yet mean arrest — it means augment first.
  4. Check the FHR category: must be Category I to call the tracing "reassuring" in your assessment — if it's II or III, that becomes part of the assessment, not a footnote.
  5. Check maternal status: afebrile, vitals stable, pain reasonably controlled — a normal assessment requires this to be true too, not just the cervix/tracing.
  6. Conclude: if change is occurring (even slowly) and hasn't hit the arrest threshold, contractions are adequate, tracing is Category I, and mom is stable → "progressing appropriately." If any one of these fails, that failure IS your assessment (e.g., "arrest of dilation" or "Category II tracing" becomes the lead, not an aside).
Notice the pattern: check phase → check rate/trend against a real threshold → check the gating factor (adequate contractions) → check FHR → check maternal status → THEN conclude. Skipping straight to "she's progressing well" without checking the threshold and the gating factor is guessing, not assessing.

When You're Not Sure — How to Frame Uncertainty

  • Don't skip the assessment because you're unsure — state your leading diagnosis and why
  • Formula: "This is most consistent with [X] given [key finding], though I'd also consider [Y] given [reason] — [what would distinguish them]"
  • It's fine (and expected as a student) to have a shorter differential than a resident — 1-2 named possibilities with your reasoning beats a long unranked list

Note Templates — Admission, Progress, Mag Check, Tracing Check

General Framework — Every L&D Note

  • SOAP for progress-type notes (labor progress, Mag checks, tracing checks): Subjective → Objective → Assessment → Plan
  • H&P format for admission notes: HPI → OB/GYN/Med/Surg/Social/Family history → Meds/Allergies → relevant labs → Exam → numbered Assessment/Plan (one numbered problem per active issue)
  • Every plan ends with: "Discussed with [resident level] resident, who agreed with above stated plan." — this is a required line, not optional, since a student can't independently order things
  • Objective vitals are given as a current value AND a range/trend for the shift (e.g., "BP 142/89 (Shift range: 129-151/80-92)") — a single number without the trend hides whether things are worsening or stable

Mag Check Note (Preeclampsia/Eclampsia Monitoring)

S: MS3 in to see patient for examination [X] hours s/p [delivery type] complicated by [preeclampsia with/without severe features]. Currently [endorses/denies] headache, changes in vision, chest pain, shortness of breath, nausea, vomiting, and RUQ pain — [this symptom checklist is the Mag-check-specific ROS; always ask all of these, since they're the severe-feature/Mag-toxicity screen]. Pain [level]/10. [Vaginal bleeding amount/flow]. [Feeding plan status — pumping/breastfeeding, any difficulty]. O: T / HR (range) / BP (shift range) / O2 / RR I/O: [intake / output, e.g., "345 (678) / 2300 (2800)" — running total plus recent interval in parentheses] — this matters because output tracks renal clearance of Mag and volume status Lungs: CTAB Heart: RRR Abd: [fundal tenderness?] Ext: [edema] Neuro: AOx3 (Alert & Oriented x3), [reflexes — e.g., 2+], [clonus present/absent] — DTRs and clonus are the bedside screen for Mag toxicity Perineum: [pad findings] A: [age]yo G_P____, PPD#[_] s/p [delivery type] @ [EGA] complicated by [preeclampsia with severe features]. Currently with [normal/abnormal] vitals, exam, and UOP. [Any postpartum concerns, or "no concerns"]. P: Continue Mg drip @ [rate] until 24hrs postpartum. [Diet plan]. Monitor BP, UOP, and symptoms of severe preeclampsia (SPEC). Continue Mag checks Q[interval]hrs. Labs PRN. [Lactation consult if needed]. [Pain control PRN]. Discussed with [level] resident, who agreed with above stated plan.
The symptom checklist in S (headache, visual changes, chest pain, SOB, N/V, RUQ pain) and the DTR/clonus check in O are not optional filler — they're the actual clinical screen for both worsening preeclampsia and Mag toxicity. Skipping them isn't just incomplete documentation, it's missing the point of the note.

Mag Check: Interpretation → Assessment → Plan

What you foundInterpretationPlan
Normal DTRs (2+), no clonus, RR normal, UOP adequate, symptoms all negativeTherapeutic Mag level, no toxicity, no worsening preeclampsiaContinue current Mg rate, continue routine checks at the same interval
Diminished/absent DTRsEarly sign of Mag toxicity (DTRs are lost before respiratory depression)Check Mag level, consider holding/reducing the drip, notify resident — don't wait for respiratory symptoms to act
RR <12, or O2 desaturationMore advanced Mag toxicity — respiratory depressionStop the Mag drip immediately, notify resident/attending, have calcium gluconate (the antidote) ready
New headache, visual changes, RUQ pain, or worsening BP despite MagPossible progression of preeclampsia severity, not a Mag-dosing issueRecheck BP series, consider repeat labs (platelets, LFTs, Cr), notify resident — this may change the delivery timeline, not just the Mag rate
UOP declining / I/O trending negativeReduced renal clearance of Mag — risk of accumulation and toxicity even without other symptoms yetNotify resident, consider checking a Mag level, watch DTRs more closely
The through-line: DTRs and RR are your fastest bedside toxicity check (labs take time), while the symptom ROS is your fastest bedside check for whether the underlying preeclampsia is actually getting worse — these are two different questions being screened by the same note.
