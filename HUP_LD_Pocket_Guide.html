What specifically to look for on the ultrasound report

  • Dating/EDD: was it confirmed by early US — does it match what's documented as her EDD now?
  • Anatomy survey: completed? Any noted anomalies?
  • Growth (EFW + percentile): EFW = Estimated Fetal Weight, calculated by the ultrasound from measurements of the head, abdomen, and femur. It's plotted on a growth chart as a percentile for gestational age — where does it sit, and, if multiple scans exist, is the percentile trend stable or crossing down (concerning for IUGR = Intrauterine Growth Restriction)?
  • Amniotic fluid: measured as AFI (Amniotic Fluid Index — sum of the deepest fluid pocket in all 4 quadrants of the uterus) or SDP (Single Deepest Pocket — just the single largest pocket found anywhere) — is it normal, oligohydramnios (too little fluid), or polyhydramnios (too much)?
  • Placental location: clear of the internal os? Any noted previa or low-lying placenta?
  • Presentation: vertex, breech, or transverse — and is this the most recent scan (presentation can change until late in pregnancy)?
  • Dopplers (if done — usually only if growth-restricted or other risk factor): a special ultrasound mode that measures blood flow resistance. Umbilical artery Dopplers assess how easily blood flows from baby to placenta (high resistance = placental insufficiency); MCA (Middle Cerebral Artery) Dopplers assess fetal brain blood flow, which can show a compensatory "brain-sparing" pattern in a stressed fetus. Are they normal or abnormal resistance?
If you walk in already knowing dating, growth trend, fluid, placental location, and presentation, you can focus the actual interview on the chief complaint instead of re-deriving the whole pregnancy history from scratch.

Step 2: Offer to take the history

  • Don't wait to be asked — ask the PGY3/resident "can I take the history on the new patient?"
  • Use what you already pulled from the chart as your starting point — confirm rather than re-ask

Step 3: Structure the interview

  1. Chief complaint in her own words
  2. HPI specific to the complaint (see the relevant scenario tab — Bleeding, ROM, PTL, etc. — for what to ask)
  3. Confirm/update what you pulled from the chart rather than re-asking all of it
  4. Pertinent ROS (headache/visual changes, RUQ pain, fever, fetal movement)

Step 4: Present with a differential + plan, not just facts

"This is [name], a [age]yo G_P____ at [EGA] by [dating method], presenting with [chief complaint]. [HPI]. Per her chart, growth has been [appropriate/trending down], fluid [normal], placenta [clear of os / previa noted], presentation [vertex/breech]. She denies [pertinent negatives]. Exam/monitor: [findings]. This is most consistent with [assessment], and my plan would be to [__]."
Notice this pulls directly from your chart review (growth, fluid, placenta, presentation) — that's what turns a bare fact-recitation into a presentation that shows you did the prep work.

Labor Arrest Definitions

  • Active phase arrest: ≥6cm + ROM + no cervical change ≥4h with adequate contractions (or ≥6h if inadequate/augmented)
  • 2nd stage arrest, nullip: ≥3h pushing (≥4h with epidural)
  • 2nd stage arrest, multip: ≥2h pushing (≥3h with epidural)
  • Adequate contractions ≈ Montevideo units >200 (measured via IUPC — see "Other Labor-Monitoring Terms" below) or palpably strong q2–3min by exam
Friedman curve is outdated — modern (Zhang) curves show active labor often doesn't start till 6cm and progresses more slowly than classically taught.

Induction / Augmentation Agents — Explained

  • Cervical ripening — the cervix has to soften/thin/dilate somewhat before labor can be effectively induced (see Bishop Score). If it's unfavorable, you "ripen" it first with:
    • Foley balloon (mechanical): a catheter with a balloon is placed through the cervix and inflated — the balloon's pressure against the internal os physically stretches and dilates the cervix (no medication involved)
    • Misoprostol (PGE1): a prostaglandin pill placed vaginally or orally that softens/ripens the cervix and can also stimulate contractions
    • Dinoprostone (PGE2, brand name Cervidil): a prostaglandin inserted vaginally (like a small tampon-shaped insert) that ripens the cervix over several hours
  • Oxytocin (Pitocin): a synthetic version of the hormone that stimulates uterine contractions — given as a continuous IV drip that's titrated up gradually to build an effective contraction pattern; this is what most people mean by "being induced" or "augmented"
  • Amniotomy (AROM = Artificial Rupture Of Membranes): the provider uses a small hook-like tool to manually break the amniotic sac ("break the water") — this can jump-start or strengthen labor on its own, and is often done alongside Pitocin. Usually only done once the fetal head is engaged in the pelvis (so there's no space for the umbilical cord to slip down first — a cord prolapse risk)
Tachysystole = too many contractions — specifically >5 contractions in a 10-minute window, averaged over 30 minutes. This matters because contractions that are too frequent don't give the placenta/fetus enough recovery time between them, which can cause fetal heart rate changes. First step: reduce/stop Pitocin, reposition the mother, consider terbutaline (a medication that relaxes the uterine muscle to quickly calm down an overly active contraction pattern).

Other Labor-Monitoring Terms Worth Knowing

  • Montevideo units (MVUs): a way of quantifying contraction strength using an internal pressure catheter (IUPC, below) — you sum the pressure generated by each contraction above baseline over a 10-minute window. >200 MVUs is generally considered an "adequate" contraction pattern for labor to progress.
  • IUPC (Intrauterine Pressure Catheter): a thin catheter placed inside the uterus (alongside the fetus, through the cervix) that directly measures contraction strength in real numbers — used when you need to confirm contractions are truly adequate (e.g., before calling a labor arrest) rather than just estimating by feel or the external monitor
  • Toco: shorthand for tocodynamometer — the external belt/sensor on the mother's abdomen that tracks contraction frequency and relative timing (not true strength, unlike an IUPC)

Data to Collect (Triage / New Laboring Patient)

  1. Chart review first: age, GTPAL, EGA/dating, prior deliveries/complications, current pregnancy complications, prenatal care completeness, allergies, GBS status
  2. Chief complaint in patient's own words — contractions? bleeding? leaking fluid? decreased movement?
  3. HPI: onset/timing; for contractions — freq/duration/intensity, changed w/ movement/hydration; ask what brought her in specifically
  4. Pertinent ROS: headache/visual changes, RUQ pain, fever/chills, fetal movement
  5. Exam: vitals, fundal height, Leopold's, cervical exam if indicated
  6. Monitor: FHR category, toco pattern

Fetal Heart Rate & Fetal Status

Intrauterine Resuscitation — "LOOK"

  • Lateral positioning (off back)
  • Oxygen if hypoxia suspected
  • Off Pitocin — stop/reduce for tachysystole (too-frequent contractions) or Cat II/III tracing
  • Keep fluids going — IV bolus for hypotension (esp. post-epidural, since epidurals can drop maternal BP and reduce placental blood flow)
  • Also: check for cord prolapse/rapid cervical change; consider amnioinfusion (infusing sterile fluid into the uterus through a catheter to create a cushion around the umbilical cord, relieving compression) for variable decels; consider terbutaline (a medication that relaxes uterine muscle) for tachysystole

Pimping Pearl

Why is a late deceleration concerning but an early one isn't?
Late = uteroplacental insufficiency → fetal hypoxemia; early = benign head compression reflex.

Placenta Previa

  • Pathophys: abnormal implantation over/near internal os (risk: prior C-section/uterine surgery, multiparity). Bleeding as the lower segment thins/cervix effaces disrupts placental vessels — painless because non-myometrial.
  • Dx: TVUS showing placental edge relative to internal os.
  • Mgmt: pelvic rest, no intercourse/digital exams; C-section at term (~36–37w) if persistent.

Abruption

  • Pathophys: premature separation of normally-implanted placenta, usually maternal vessel rupture (risk: HTN/preeclampsia, trauma, cocaine, prior abruption) → retroplacental hematoma → myometrial irritation = pain + hypertonic contractions; can be concealed.
  • Dx: clinical (pain + bleeding + tender rigid uterus + FHR changes); US has low sensitivity — normal US does NOT rule it out.
  • Mgmt: depends on severity/FHR — reassuring + preterm may allow expectant mgmt + steroids; nonreassuring or maternal instability → emergent delivery.

Data to Collect & How to Present

  1. Amount/color of bleeding, presence/absence of pain, timing of onset
  2. Prior previa, prior C-section/uterine surgery, trauma, cocaine use, HTN history
  3. Vitals (maternal hemodynamic status), fundal tone/tenderness on exam
  4. FHR category — assess before any pelvic exam
  5. Do not perform digital exam — get placental location from chart/US first
"[Name], G_P__ at [EGA], presents with [painless/painful] vaginal bleeding, [amount], that started [timing]. [Relevant risk factors — prior previa/C-section, trauma, HTN]. Vitals [stable/concerning]. Uterus is [soft/nontender vs. rigid/tender]. FHR Category [__]. Placental location [known previa / not yet assessed — digital exam deferred]. This is most consistent with [previa/abruption/other], plan is [TVUS / expectant monitoring / prepare for delivery]."

Pimping Pearl

Why do we avoid a digital exam in suspected previa?
Can lacerate the previa and provoke catastrophic hemorrhage — use TVUS instead.

Hypertensive Disorders / Preeclampsia / HELLP

Severity Spectrum

  • Gestational HTN: BP ≥140/90 after 20w, no proteinuria/end-organ involvement
  • Preeclampsia: + proteinuria OR end-organ dysfunction
  • Preeclampsia with severe features: BP ≥160/110 (x2, ≥4h apart), headache unresponsive to Tylenol, visual changes, RUQ/epigastric pain, plt <100k, Cr >1.1 or 2x baseline, LFTs ≥2x normal, pulmonary edema
One severe feature alone is enough to call "severe" — proteinuria isn't required if a severe feature is present.

Pathophys

Abnormal placental spiral artery remodeling → placental ischemia → release of anti-angiogenic factors (sFlt-1, soluble endoglin) → systemic endothelial dysfunction → vasoconstriction, ↑vascular permeability, microthrombi. HELLP = microangiopathic hemolysis + hepatic microthrombi/ischemia + platelet consumption from endothelial injury — classically presents like "gastroenteritis" (n/v, RUQ pain).

Management

  • Mag sulfate — seizure prophylaxis (NMDA antagonism/cerebral vasodilation)
  • Antihypertensives if severe range (labetalol/hydralazine/nifedipine)
  • Definitive tx = delivery — severe features → deliver ≥34w; without severe features → can go to 37w
  • Eclamptic seizure → ABCs first, then Mag (NOT antiepileptics like phenytoin)
Mag toxicity: loss of DTRs → respiratory depression → cardiac arrest. Antidote = calcium gluconate.

Data to Collect & How to Present

  1. BP trend (not just one reading) — pull last several values
  2. Symptom screen: headache, visual changes, RUQ/epigastric pain
  3. Labs: CBC (platelets), LFTs, Cr, urine protein:Cr ratio
  4. DTRs/clonus if on Mag; I/Os if on Mag (renal clearance)
  5. Fetal status: FHR category, growth/fluid if IUGR concern
"[Name], G_P__ at [EGA], with [gestational HTN/preeclampsia], BP trending [__/__], [with/without severe-range readings]. She [endorses/denies] headache, visual changes, RUQ pain. Labs show platelets [__], Cr [__], LFTs [__]. [On Mag — DTRs intact, no clonus, I/Os adequate]. FHR Category [__]. This [does/does not] meet criteria for severe features, so the plan is [continue Mag + BP control / deliver given severe features and GA / expectant management to 37w]."

Pimping Pearl

Why do we give Mag for preeclampsia and not an antiepileptic?
Mag is proven specifically for eclamptic seizure prophylaxis/treatment (MAGPIE trial); antiepileptics like phenytoin are not standard and are a classic wrong-answer trap.

ROM / PPROM / Chorioamnionitis

Differential — Pertinent Positives / Negatives

Pathophys & Diagnosis

Pathophys: membrane weakening, often subclinical ascending infection/inflammation (esp. preterm) or mechanical stress; once ruptured, loss of the sterile barrier allows ascending flora → chorioamnionitis.

Dx — ROM: nitrazine (amniotic fluid alkaline → blue), ferning (estrogen-rich fluid crystallizes), pooling.

Dx — Chorio: maternal fever + one of (fetal tachycardia, maternal leukocytosis, uterine tenderness, purulent fluid).

Management

  • Term PROM → induce labor
  • PPROM <34w, no infection → expectant mgmt + latency abx (ampicillin/azithro) + steroids (lung maturity) + Mag if <32w (neuroprotection)
  • PPROM + chorio, any GA → deliver + broad-spectrum abx (ampicillin + gentamicin ± clinda/azithro) regardless of gestational age

Data to Collect & How to Present

  1. Fluid description: gush vs. trickle, color, odor, ongoing vs. one-time; timing
  2. Exam findings: nitrazine, ferning, pooling (all three if possible)
  3. Temp, uterine tenderness, fetal tachycardia — chorio screen
  4. EGA — determines management pathway (term vs. PPROM, <32w vs. 32-34w)
  5. GBS status if known
"[Name], G_P__ at [EGA], presents with [gush/leaking] of fluid since [timing]. Exam [+/−nitrazine, +/−ferning, +/−pooling]. She is [afebrile/febrile], uterus [nontender/tender], FHR [reassuring/tachycardic]. GBS status [__]. This is consistent with [SROM/PPROM] [with/without signs of chorio]. Given her GA of [__], plan is [induce / expectant mgmt with latency abx + steroids ± Mag / deliver now given chorio]."

Pimping Pearl

Why does chorio management not depend on mode of delivery?
Antibiotics + delivery are the treatment; C-section is not automatically indicated just because of chorio — reserved for standard obstetric indications.

Preterm Labor

Differential — Pertinent Positives / Negatives

DxPertinent (+)Pertinent (−)
True preterm laborRegular painful contractions <37w AND cervical change on serial exams—
Braxton-HicksIrregular, non-progressive; often relieved by hydration/rest/positionNo cervical change, resolves with time
PPROM+Nitrazine/ferning/pooling regardless of contractions—
Round ligament/MSK painPositional, sharp, unilateral, reproducible w/ movementNo regular contraction pattern, no cervical change

Fetal fibronectin (22–34w, membranes intact, minimal dilation) and TVUS cervical length both have strong negative predictive value to rule OUT true preterm labor.

Pathophys & Diagnosis

Multifactorial — subclinical infection/inflammation, uterine overdistension, cervical insufficiency, or vascular pathology (abruption) → final common pathway of prostaglandin release → cervical ripening + contractions before 37w.

Dx: regular contractions + objective cervical change on serial exam.

Management (24–34w)

  • Corticosteroids (betamethasone) — accelerates fetal surfactant production
  • Mag sulfate if <32w — neuroprotection
  • Tocolytics (nifedipine, indomethacin) — buy 48h for steroids to work, NOT long-term suppression
Avoid tocolytics if chorio, severe preeclampsia, or nonreassuring fetal status. Avoid indomethacin >32w (risk of premature ductus arteriosus closure).

Data to Collect & How to Present

  1. Contraction pattern: freq/regularity, and whether it's changed with hydration/rest
  2. Cervical exam — serial if possible, to confirm change
  3. fFN and/or cervical length if done
  4. Rule-outs: UA (UTI), ROM triad (nitrazine/ferning/pooling), fever/uterine tenderness (chorio)
  5. GA — determines whether steroids/Mag/tocolytics apply
"[Name], G_P__ at [EGA], presents with [regular/irregular] contractions since [timing]. Cervical exam shows [__cm/__%eff], [changed/unchanged from prior]. [fFN positive/negative, cervical length __mm if done]. Afebrile, uterus nontender, UA [unremarkable]. FHR reassuring. This is [consistent with/against] true preterm labor. Given GA of [__], plan is [steroids + Mag + tocolysis / reassurance and discharge / continued observation]."

Pimping Pearls

Why give betamethasone before 34 weeks but generally not after?
Benefit (reduced RDS/neonatal morbidity) is well established <34w; benefit still shown 34-36+6w in some patients (ALPS trial) but weighed against risk of neonatal hypoglycemia.
Why avoid indomethacin after 32 weeks?
Risk of premature closure of the fetal ductus arteriosus increases with advancing gestational age.

Decreased Fetal Movement

Differential — Pertinent Positives / Negatives

DxPertinent (+)Pertinent (−)
Benign/normal variantReassuring NST/BPP, normal fluid, resolves w/ repositioning/hydrationNo risk factors, no bleeding/pain

Workup: NST (Non-Stress Test) first — a 20+ minute strip of fetal heart monitoring looking for reassuring accelerations without needing any stimulus ("non-stress" = not provoking contractions). If non-reassuring, follow with BPP (Biophysical Profile) — an ultrasound-based scoring system checking fetal breathing movements, body movements, tone, and amniotic fluid (each worth 2 points, plus the NST itself, out of a total of 10) — and/or umbilical artery Dopplers.

Data to Collect & How to Present

  1. Baseline movement pattern vs. today — when did she last feel normal movement
  2. Any interventions tried (juice, lying on side) and response
  3. Risk factors: growth concerns, oligo/polyhydramnios, prior stillbirth
  4. NST result; BPP/Dopplers if NST non-reassuring
"[Name], G_P__ at [EGA], presents with decreased fetal movement since [timing], [unchanged/improved] despite [hydration/repositioning]. No bleeding or pain. NST is [reactive/nonreactive]. [If further workup: BPP score __, Dopplers __]. This is [reassuring/concerning], plan is [discharge with kick counts / further antenatal testing / admit for closer monitoring]."

Pimping Pearl

Why does a reassuring NST not fully rule out fetal compromise?
NST has a high false-positive (nonreassuring) rate but a reassuring NST has excellent negative predictive value for the short term — it doesn't evaluate chronic processes like growth restriction, hence pairing with growth scans/Dopplers when indicated.

Delivery Day — 2nd/3rd Stage & Shoulder Dystocia

Called: Delivery Imminent — Your Role

  • Go immediately — gown + gloves on even before the room/table is ready
  • Position where directed (usually beside, not in front)
  • Help with comfort/positioning: ice chips, heat pack, holding legs
  • Gown on delivery table + pull your own gloves early — have ready before table is set up
  • May help hand-over-hand with infant delivery and/or placenta
  • Help retract during vaginal repairs
  • Don't grab without being invited — watch for cues

Shoulder Dystocia — Pathophys → Dx → Mgmt

Pathophys: anterior shoulder impacts maternal pubic symphysis after head delivers (risk: macrosomia/GDM, prior dystocia, prolonged 2nd stage, operative delivery). Bony obstruction, not power problem — more pushing/fundal pressure worsens impaction.

Dx: turtle sign (head retracts against perineum), failure of restitution, difficulty delivering anterior shoulder.

Mgmt — HELPERR:

  • Help — call for help
  • Evaluate for episiotomy
  • Legs — McRoberts maneuver: sharply flex the mother's hips up toward her chest (like pulling her knees to her shoulders) — this rotates the pelvis and flattens the curve of the lower spine, widening the effective pelvic outlet
  • Pressure — suprapubic pressure: an assistant pushes down/inward just above the mother's pubic bone to push the baby's shoulder out from behind it (different from fundal pressure at the top of the uterus, which is NOT used here)
  • Enter maneuvers — internal rotation (Woods screw / Rubin): the provider reaches in and manually rotates the baby's shoulders from the front-to-back (AP) diameter into an angled position, which is roomier — Woods screw rotates by pushing the posterior shoulder, Rubin by pushing the anterior shoulder
  • Remove the posterior arm — the provider reaches in, locates the baby's back (posterior) arm, and sweeps it out across the chest and delivers it first, which effectively narrows the shoulder width and frees the impacted shoulder
  • Roll the patient — Gaskin maneuver: repositioning the mother onto her hands and knees (all-fours) — gravity and the change in pelvic dimensions in this position can dislodge the impacted shoulder
Document time from head to body delivery.

Pimping Pearls

Why does fundal pressure make shoulder dystocia worse?
It's a bony impaction problem, not a power problem — more pushing force wedges the shoulder further against the symphysis.
Why does operative vaginal delivery require specific criteria?
Operative vaginal delivery = using forceps or a vacuum device to assist delivery of the baby's head during the second stage, instead of a spontaneous push-only delivery. Because of the risks involved (maternal laceration, fetal injury), it requires confirmed criteria first: full cervical dilation, ruptured membranes, known fetal station/head position, adequate pain control, an empty maternal bladder, and willingness to abandon the attempt and move to C-section if it isn't working.

C-Section

Common Indications

  • Arrest of dilation/descent (failed induction or labor arrest)
  • Category III / non-reassuring tracing not resolved by resuscitation
  • Malpresentation (breech, transverse)
  • Placenta previa / vasa previa
  • Repeat (prior classical or ≥2 prior low transverse, or maternal request)
  • Cord prolapse (emergent)
  • Active herpes lesion, certain maternal medical conditions

Your Role

  • Confirm you're actually scrubbing (vs. observing) before gowning
  • Pull up H&P/indication en route if possible
  • Introduce yourself to the patient in pre-op/holding if you can catch her
  • Read chart/H&P beforehand
  • Graded responsibility: suture-cutting → retraction → skin suturing/knot-tying
  • Limit talking — patient is awake
Do not scrub into a STAT section unless instructed.

Postpartum Hemorrhage

4 T's — Pathophys → Dx → Management

Cause%Pathophys / Key Clue
Tone~70-80%Myometrium fails to contract on spiral arteries after separation (risk: overdistended uterus, prolonged labor, chorio, grand multip) → boggy uterus
Trauma~20%Mechanical disruption — laceration, hematoma, uterine rupture/inversion
Tissue~10%Retained placenta/clots prevent contraction over that segment — "missing portion" on inspection
Thrombin<1%Coagulopathy (DIC from abruption/AFE) — fibrinogen drops first/fastest
Definition: ≥1000mL blood loss (any route) or signs/symptoms of hypovolemia, regardless of delivery mode.

Atony Management Ladder

  1. Bimanual uterine massage
  2. Oxytocin (first line)
  3. Methylergonovine — avoid in HTN
  4. Carboprost/Hemabate — avoid in asthma
  5. Misoprostol — another prostaglandin uterotonic, can be given rectally/orally/sublingually
  6. TXA (tranexamic acid): a medication that helps blood clot by blocking the breakdown of clots (antifibrinolytic) — doesn't make the uterus contract, but reduces bleeding as an adjunct
  7. Escalate if bleeding continues despite the above:
    • Uterine balloon tamponade (e.g., Bakri balloon) — a balloon device is placed inside the uterus and inflated, applying direct pressure to the bleeding uterine wall from the inside, like packing a wound
    • B-Lynch suture — a large suture placed around the outside of the uterus during surgery that compresses it like a tight brace, mechanically squeezing it closed
    • Uterine artery embolization — an interventional radiology procedure that blocks off the blood vessels feeding the uterus
    • Hysterectomy — surgical removal of the uterus; last resort when all else fails

Treat the specific "T" you find: repair lacerations, remove retained tissue, correct coagulopathy with blood products.

Data to Collect & How to Present (in real time, during PPH)

  1. Cumulative EBL — running total, not just "a lot"
  2. Fundal tone on exam (boggy = atony) — check first, it's the most common cause
  3. Inspect for lacerations, retained placenta fragments
  4. Vitals trend — HR/BP for hemodynamic status
  5. Interventions already given and response

Pimping Pearls

Why is fibrinogen checked first in suspected obstetric coagulopathy?
Fibrinogen is the first factor to drop and drops disproportionately in obstetric hemorrhage/DIC (e.g., abruption, AFE) compared to other pregnancies' baseline elevated fibrinogen.
Why is oxytocin first-line for atony over ergot/prostaglandin agents?
Best safety profile, fewest contraindications, and standard first-line per ACOG — other agents reserved for refractory cases or when oxytocin is contraindicated/insufficient.

Assessment Framework — How to Write/Say the "A"

The General Formula

[Age] G_P____ at [EGA] [+ key risk factor if relevant], [admitted for / presenting with __], now [current status / key finding], concerning for OR consistent with [diagnosis/problem]. [If uncertain: "differential includes X vs Y, favoring X given __."]
  • An assessment is a synthesis, not a diagnosis label alone — it should show your reasoning, not just name a disease
  • Formula: Identifier + relevant context → current key finding → your interpretation
  • If it's a normal/reassuring situation, say so explicitly — "reassuring," "appropriate," "uncomplicated" — don't just go silent on the assessment because nothing is wrong
  • One line is usually enough; add a differential only when the diagnosis is genuinely uncertain
The test of a good assessment: could someone who only heard your assessment (not your whole presentation) know what's going on and what you think is happening? If not, it's still just data.

When You're Not Sure — How to Frame Uncertainty

  • Don't skip the assessment because you're unsure — state your leading diagnosis and why
  • Formula: "This is most consistent with [X] given [key finding], though I'd also consider [Y] given [reason] — [what would distinguish them]"
  • It's fine (and expected as a student) to have a shorter differential than a resident — 1-2 named possibilities with your reasoning beats a long unranked list
