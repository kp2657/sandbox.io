How to use this tool

Every ROS question you ask should help you build or narrow a differential diagnosis. A pertinent positive is a finding the patient endorses that supports a specific diagnosis. A pertinent negative is a finding the patient denies that helps exclude a diagnosis. When you document your ROS, include both — they show your clinical reasoning.
Example — 4-year-old with fever and rash:
Pertinent positive: "Sandpaper-textured rash on trunk" → supports scarlet fever
Pertinent negative: "Denies oral lesions, conjunctivitis, extremity edema" → argues against Kawasaki disease

Summary: how to document pertinent positives & negatives

Your ROS should NOT be a laundry list of "denies" for every system. Instead: (1) identify your top 2–3 differential diagnoses based on the chief complaint, (2) ask targeted ROS questions that would confirm or exclude those diagnoses, (3) document the pertinent positives (findings supporting your leading diagnosis), and (4) document the pertinent negatives (findings that help rule out dangerous or likely alternatives).
Example note — 3-year-old with fever and cough:
ROS: Per mother, fever to 39.5°C x2 days with wet cough and tachypnea (pertinent positives for pneumonia). Denies stridor, barking cough (argues against croup). Denies ear pain or tugging (argues against AOM). Denies dysuria or foul-smelling urine (argues against UTI as fever source). Denies rash, petechiae (argues against meningococcemia). Denies neck stiffness, photophobia, altered mental status (argues against meningitis). Feeding well with adequate urine output (reassuring against dehydration and serious bacterial infection).
This approach demonstrates clinical reasoning and helps your attending understand your thought process.
