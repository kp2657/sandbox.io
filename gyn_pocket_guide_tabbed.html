🩺 Benign Gyn Pocket Guide

Quick-reference for the rotation

Before Every OR Case

  • Meet assigned resident in pre-op ~6:50–7am for first patient
  • Know chief complaint + surgical indication before scrubbing in
  • Confirm Pelvic EUA consent signed
  • If consented, remind team to teach bimanual/speculum exam
  • Carelign made the night before (if major/overnight case)

During the Case

  • Assist with retraction, uterine manipulation, closing ports
  • Note EBL, UOP, IVF for Carelign update after
  • Ask a prepped question or float a differential when appropriate

After the Case

  • Update Carelign: EBL, UOP, IVF
  • Set POC (postop check) time — 4–6 hrs after leaving OR

Before Rounds (AM)

  • Read one-liner in Carelign — understand what surgery + why
  • Check with intern for overnight verbal updates not yet charted
  • Pull vitals, UOP, drain output (last 12 hrs)
  • Pull labs — know the POD1 Hgb trend + EBL context
  • Pre-round if possible: pain, ambulation, voiding, diet tolerance, flatus
  • Do pertinent exam (abdomen, incision, foley, JP output)
  • Review Carelign plan by system — form your own opinion on any updates

Weekly / Recurring

  • Check Friday email for next week's OR assignments
  • Review any pre-circulated articles before Tuesday preop conference
  • Prep Thursday Quantbook 5–10 min article presentation
  • Help with discharge summaries as needed

Key Pelvic FasciaThe dissection planes, not just the ligaments

Ligaments are discrete cord-like structures you clamp/cut. Fascia is more like connective-tissue "sheets" you develop dissection planes through — this is the tissue you're actually separating during bladder flap and posterior dissection.

FasciaLocationClinical relevance
Vesicouterine (vesicocervical) fasciaBetween the bladder and the anterior uterus/cervixThe plane developed when creating the bladder flap during hysterectomy — separating the bladder off the lower uterine segment/cervix. Thin here in patients with prior C-sections (scarring fuses the planes), raising bladder injury risk.
Rectovaginal fascia (Denonvilliers' fascia)Between the posterior vaginal wall and rectumThe plane developed posteriorly, especially relevant when the posterior cul-de-sac is obliterated by endometriosis — dissecting the rectum off the posterior vagina/cervix here is one of the highest-risk steps for bowel injury in the endo case.
Endopelvic fasciaGeneral connective tissue investing pelvic organs, condensing to form the cardinal/uterosacral ligament complexNot a single sheet — think of it as the fibromuscular "matrix" the named ligaments are thickenings of. Provides overall pelvic organ support (relevant to pelvic organ prolapse, less so to your current case list).
Presacral fasciaOverlying the sacrum, posterior to the rectumRelevant if dissection extends deep posteriorly (e.g., rectosigmoid mobilization for CRS-involved endo cases) — presacral venous plexus here can bleed significantly if injured.
Where this shows up in your cases
  • Every TLH — vesicouterine fascia must be divided to create the bladder flap before uterine vessel ligation
  • Endo/CRS case — rectovaginal fascia dissection is the critical (and highest bowel-injury-risk) step when the cul-de-sac is obliterated; presacral fascia relevant if bowel is mobilized further

Pimping-ready line: "Ligaments are what you clamp and cut; fascia is what you dissect through to get there safely."

AUB Decision Tree
1. Risk stratify Postmenopausal bleeding (PMB) → any bleeding abnormal, r/o cancer.
Reproductive age → r/o pregnancy first, then PALM-COEIN.
↓
2. Imaging (TVUS ± saline sonogram) Thick stripe → sample. Focal lesion → polyp. Submucosal mass → fibroid. Irregular contour → adhesions/retained POC. Normal → likely non-structural.
↓
3. OR vs. medical mgmt Surgery if: cancer risk (PMB/thick stripe) OR structural lesion that's diagnostic+treatable via hysteroscopy.
No structural cause + low risk → trial medical (hormonal) management first.
↓
4. In the OR — "see and treat" Diagnostic hysteroscopy visualizes cavity → findings dictate add-on:
  • Polyp → polypectomy
  • Submucosal fibroid → hysteroscopic myomectomy
  • Adhesions → lysis of adhesions (LOA)
  • Nothing focal → D&C alone (tissue dx)

Core logic: hysteroscopy = diagnostic; D&C = diagnostic + therapeutic. The procedure is whatever hysteroscopy reveals.

Hysteroscopy Fluid Deficit SafetyRelevant for every HSC D&C case
  • Electrolyte-poor media (e.g., glycine/sorbitol) — used with monopolar energy. Stop case at ~1000 mL deficit (risk of hyponatremia/hypervolemia).
  • Electrolyte-rich media (normal saline) — used with bipolar energy (most current resectoscopes). Safer; stop at ~2500 mL deficit in healthy patients, lower (~1000 mL) if cardiac/renal comorbidity.
  • Circulating nurse/anesthesia tracks inflow vs. outflow continuously — ask what the running deficit is if the case is running long (fibroid resections take longer than polypectomies).
Cystoscopy InterpretationEnd-of-case check for TLH/BS
  • Bladder mucosa — should be smooth, no suture, no thermal injury, no filling defect.
  • Ureteral jets — bilateral efflux of urine (sometimes IV indigo carmine or fluorescein used to enhance visibility) confirms ureteral patency — the key safety check after hysterectomy dissection near the ureters.
  • Absent/sluggish jet on one side → concern for ureteral kinking, ligation, or injury → may prompt intraop urology consult.

Preop Optimization FrameworkWhy patients get PAT, T&S DOS, or med holds
  • T&S at PAT vs. DOS: Higher anticipated blood loss (TLH, myomectomy, endo/CRS cases) → often T&S or type & cross done ahead at PAT so blood is ready. Lower-risk cases (HSC D&C alone) → T&S same-day is usually sufficient.
  • New H&P needed: Most institutions require H&P within ~30 days of surgery — if the case date has slipped, a new one is required before OR day.
  • GLP-1 agonists (Mounjaro, etc.): Held preop per ASA guidance — delayed gastric emptying → aspiration risk under anesthesia. Confirm hold duration (often 1 week for weekly formulations).
  • Preop misoprostol: Given for hysteroscopic cases to ripen the cervix and ease dilation, especially nulliparous patients or anticipated difficult entry.
  • Bowel prep: Reserved for cases with anticipated bowel involvement (deep endometriosis with CRS co-management) — not routine for standard hysterectomy/myomectomy.
  • MRI preop: Ordered for fibroid mapping when precise size/location changes the surgical plan (e.g., choosing LSC mmx vs. TLH, or planning a complex myomectomy).
Preop To-Do PatternsWhat those checkboxes mean
  • New H&P — history & physical needs updating before surgery (usually if last one is aging out, ~30 day rule).
  • T&S DOS vs. PAT appt — Type & Screen: either done day-of-surgery or at a separate pre-admission testing (PAT) visit, depending on case complexity/blood loss risk.
  • Consents — surgical consent must list every planned procedure (e.g., "add polyp," "add endo," CRS consents) — if a possible add-on isn't consented, it can't be done intraop.
  • EUA consent — separate consent for student pelvic exam under anesthesia.
  • Preop misoprostol (miso) — given before hysteroscopy to soften/ripen the cervix, easing dilation.
  • HOLD medications — GLP-1 agonists (Mounjaro, etc.) held preop (aspiration risk from delayed gastric emptying); other home meds per anesthesia/surgeon protocol.
  • TXA — tranexamic acid, sometimes given to reduce intraop blood loss (esp. myomectomy).
  • Bowel prep — required when colorectal surgery (CRS) involvement is anticipated (e.g., deep endometriosis with bowel disease).
  • MRI — often obtained preop for fibroid mapping (size/location) to plan surgical approach.
  • Mirena/IUD ordered — placed at time of D&C for ongoing AUB management post-procedure.
🩸 Fibroids → TLH, BS, cysto
Anatomy
  • Fibroids (leiomyomas) = benign monoclonal smooth muscle tumors of the myometrium
  • TLH chosen when fibroids are intramural/subserosal, large, multiple, or cavity distortion too extensive for uterine-sparing surgery
  • Ureters — cross under the uterine artery ~1–2cm lateral to the cervix ("water under the bridge")
  • Uterine artery — branches off internal iliac
  • Bladder — dissected off the lower uterine segment/cervix
  • Round ligament — runs from the uterine cornu, through the inguinal canal, to the labia majora; contains Sampson's artery (small vessel, needs ligation). First structure typically transected — divide it to open the broad ligament and access the pelvic sidewall/retroperitoneum.
  • Infundibulopelvic (IP) ligament / suspensory ligament of the ovary — contains the ovarian artery and vein (ovarian artery = direct branch of the aorta), plus lymphatics and nerve fibers. Runs from the pelvic sidewall to the ovary. Only relevant here if ovaries are also being removed — if ovaries are conserved, the utero-ovarian ligament is transected instead (connects ovary to uterine cornu, contains a small anastomotic vessel).
  • The ureter runs just medial/deep to the IP ligament as it crosses the pelvic brim — a key reason to identify the ureter before clamping the IP ligament if oophorectomy is being performed.
Indications
  • Symptomatic fibroids (bleeding, bulk symptoms, pain), childbearing complete
  • Failed or declined medical/uterine-sparing management
  • Very large or numerous fibroids
  • Suspected leiomyosarcoma (rare — rapid growth or postmenopausal growth raises concern)
Medical management (tried first)
  • NSAIDs, tranexamic acid for bleeding
  • Hormonal: combined OCPs, progestin IUD, GnRH agonists/antagonists (leuprolide, elagolix) — shrink fibroids, often a bridge to surgery or to correct anemia preop
Surgical steps — structures to identify first
  • Before any cutting: trace the ureter along the pelvic sidewall (visualize peristalsis if possible) — do this before touching the round ligament or IP ligament
  • Identify the uterine vessels at the level of the internal os before skeletonizing
  • Identify the bladder reflection/peritoneal fold before developing the bladder flap
Surgical steps — sequence

Port placement → transect round ligament (opens broad ligament) → transect/ligate tube ± utero-ovarian ligament or IP ligament (depending on whether ovaries are conserved) → develop bladder flap by incising the vesicouterine fascia to separate the bladder off the lower uterine segment/cervix → skeletonize and ligate uterine arteries at the level of the internal os → transect cardinal ligaments (lateral) then uterosacral ligaments (posterior) → colpotomy (open vaginal cuff circumferentially) → deliver specimen → close vaginal cuff → cystoscopy.

Blood supply — what gets divided
  • Uterine artery — main supply, branch of the internal iliac (anterior division); identified and ligated at the level of the internal os, right where it crosses over the ureter ("water under the bridge") — the single highest-stakes vessel in the case
  • Ovarian artery (via IP ligament) — only divided if oophorectomy performed; direct branch off the aorta. If ovaries conserved, only the smaller utero-ovarian vessel is divided instead
  • Vaginal artery contributions — anastomose with the uterine artery's descending cervicovaginal branch; encountered near colpotomy
  • Sampson's artery — small vessel in the round ligament, ligated when the round ligament is transected
  • Uterine size/fibroid bulk increases collateral vascularity — larger uteri bleed more from these same vessels, so expect more careful hemostasis with bigger specimens
Complications
  • Ureteral injury (transection, thermal, kinking) — why cystoscopy is done
  • Bladder injury, esp. with prior C-sections/scarring
  • Hemorrhage from uterine vessels
  • Vaginal cuff dehiscence (delayed)
  • VTE — pneumatic compression intraop, early ambulation postop
Pimping-ready facts
  • Fibroids are estrogen/progesterone-responsive — regress after menopause
  • Higher incidence and earlier onset in Black patients
  • Opportunistic BS reduces ovarian cancer risk — most high-grade serous cancers arise from the fallopian tube fimbria, not the ovary
  • Cystoscopy checks bilateral ureteral jets (± indigo carmine/fluorescein) — absent jet → concern for injury
Your role — what to do
  • Pull the MRI beforehand — know fibroid size/location before scrubbing in
  • Confirm EUA consent status and T&S/PAT plan
  • In the OR: help with uterine manipulation, watch vessel ligation and ureter identification closely
  • At the end: watch cystoscopy — try to identify the ureteral jets yourself before someone points them out
  • Postop: track EBL/UOP/IVF for Carelign; know her Hgb trend for rounds
🩸 Fibroids → LSC mmxUterine-sparing
Anatomy
  • Targets intramural/subserosal fibroids (FIGO 3–7) too deep/exophytic for hysteroscopic removal
  • Myometrium incised over fibroid → pseudocapsule developed → fibroid enucleated → closed in layers
  • Round ligament — not typically transected in myomectomy (uterus is preserved in place) but serves as a key surface landmark for orienting anterior vs. posterior uterine fibroids and for avoiding the adjacent uterine vessels running along the broad ligament
  • Anterior/fundal fibroids: incision planned to avoid the tubes/round ligament insertion at the cornu; posterior fibroids: watch for proximity to the uterosacral ligaments and rectum
Indications
  • Symptomatic fibroids, patient desires future fertility or wants to retain uterus
  • Contraindicated if malignancy suspected (rapid growth, postmenopausal enlargement)
Medical management
  • GnRH agonists preop sometimes used to shrink fibroid/reduce vascularity before surgery
  • Not a substitute for removal if cavity-distorting or symptomatic and fertility desired
Surgical steps — structures to identify first
  • Confirm fibroid location/orientation relative to the cavity (via preop imaging or intraop ultrasound) before incising — determines single vs. multiple incisions and whether the cavity will be entered
  • Identify the uterine vessels along the lateral broad ligament to avoid inadvertent injury during deep enucleation
Surgical steps — sequence

Vasopressin/vasoconstrictor injected into the myometrium overlying the fibroid (reduces bleeding) → serosal/myometrial incision made down to the fibroid pseudocapsule → enucleate the fibroid with traction (tenaculum/corkscrew) and countertraction (scissors/energy along the capsule plane) → inspect for cavity breach (if entered, closure includes reapproximating endometrium separately) → multi-layer closure: deep myometrium, superficial myometrium, then serosa (imbricating layers reduces dead space and hematoma risk) → morcellation in a containment bag for specimen removal. TXA often given systemically.

Blood supply — what gets divided
  • No named vessel is deliberately ligated up front (unlike TLH) — bleeding here comes from the myometrial vasculature feeding the fibroid pseudocapsule, branches of the arcuate arteries (which arise from the uterine artery and encircle the myometrium)
  • Vasopressin injection is the main proactive bleeding-control step — it's a vasoconstrictor injected locally into the myometrium/pseudocapsule before incision, not a vessel ligation
  • Larger/deeper fibroids have a richer pseudocapsule blood supply — expect more bleeding and more energy/suture use for hemostasis as fibroid size increases
  • If bleeding is significant and uncontrolled locally, next step is often uterine artery occlusion (temporary clips/tourniquet on the uterine vessels) before continuing — a good thing to ask about if the case is bleeding more than expected
Complications
  • Significant intraop bleeding — myometrium is vascular
  • Future uterine rupture risk if closure inadequate → often counseled toward C-section
  • Conversion to laparotomy if bleeding uncontrolled
  • Adhesion formation affecting future fertility
Pimping-ready facts
  • "Enucleation" = removal along the pseudocapsule, not through healthy tissue
  • Whether the cavity was entered determines future delivery counseling
  • Power morcellation risks disseminating an occult sarcoma — hence containment bags
Your role — what to do
  • Confirm TXA/cysto plan noted for this case beforehand
  • In the OR: watch enucleation technique and multi-layer closure — good chance to ask about future delivery counseling
  • Track EBL closely — bleeding risk is higher here than in a straightforward TLH
🔴 AUB-P → HSC D&C, polypectomy
Anatomy
  • Endometrial polyps = focal overgrowths of endometrial glands/stroma around a central vascular ("feeder") vessel
  • Can be sessile (broad base) or pedunculated (on a stalk)
Indications
  • Symptomatic polyp (AUB, intermenstrual bleeding) on TVUS/saline sonography
  • Asymptomatic polyps in postmenopausal women or with risk factors (tamoxifen, obesity) often removed given malignancy risk
  • Infertility workup finding a polyp
Medical management
  • Polyps don't respond to hormonal therapy the way fibroids do
  • Progestins may reduce bleeding symptoms but don't resolve the polyp — mechanical removal is definitive
Surgical steps

Cervical dilation (± preop miso) → hysteroscope + distension media → cavity survey → polyp resected at base (scissors/graspers/morcellator) → D&C samples remainder → specimens sent separately if possible.

Blood supply — what to consider
  • Each polyp has a single central feeder vessel (a basal-layer arteriole) — this is usually what's actually causing the abnormal bleeding, not diffuse endometrial bleeding
  • Resecting the polyp at its base (not just shaving the visible portion) is what controls the feeder vessel and prevents recurrence/bleeding
  • Mechanical morcellator systems typically achieve hemostasis as they cut; if using scissors/graspers instead, brief point-cautery may be needed at the base
  • No named artery is at risk here (unlike TLH) — bleeding is local and endometrial, not from major pelvic vessels
Complications
  • Uterine perforation (higher risk with stenotic/flexed cervix)
  • Excess fluid absorption (see Frameworks tab)
  • Incomplete resection — base missed, recurrence
  • Postop intrauterine adhesions (rare, single polypectomy)
Pimping-ready facts
  • Malignant transformation ~0–13% of polyps, risk rises with age/postmenopausal status
  • Tamoxifen is a known risk factor for polyp formation
  • "See and treat" = diagnostic + therapeutic in one setting — good answer if asked why hysteroscopy is favored
Your role — what to do
  • Check if preop miso was given/needed
  • Confirm consent explicitly lists "polypectomy," not just D&C
  • In the OR: ask the running fluid deficit at some point — good habit
  • Quick case — good one to ask a focused question about "why hysteroscopy vs. blind D&C alone" if there's downtime
🔴 AUB-L → HSC D&C, mmx
Anatomy
  • Submucosal fibroid (FIGO 0/1/2) bulging into the cavity, covered by thin/often ulcerated endometrium — why submucosal fibroids bleed disproportionately to size
Indications
  • AUB attributed to a submucosal fibroid on imaging
  • Infertility/recurrent pregnancy loss with a submucosal fibroid distorting the cavity
Medical management
  • Progestin IUD, GnRH agonists/antagonists, TXA can reduce bleeding
  • Mechanical removal usually needed for definitive treatment, especially if fertility desired
Surgical steps

Resectoscope with cutting loop shaves fibroid into strips, removed as chips. Deeper (type 2) fibroids may need staged procedures.

Blood supply — what to consider
  • Submucosal fibroids are supplied by myometrial arcuate/radial artery branches feeding into the fibroid pseudocapsule from beneath — cutting into the fibroid opens these small vessels
  • Bipolar/monopolar cutting loop energy provides simultaneous cutting and coagulation — this is why bleeding is usually well-controlled during active resection, but can increase once the fibroid is fully removed and the underlying myometrial bed is exposed
  • Deeper (type 2) fibroids expose more of the myometrial vascular bed, which is part of why they carry higher fluid intravasation risk (open venous sinuses absorb distension fluid more readily) — vessels and fluid deficit are directly linked here
  • No named major artery (uterine artery itself is not approached) — all bleeding control is local, via the resectoscope's energy source
Complications
  • Fluid overload/hyponatremia (glycine) or fluid overload alone (saline)
  • Uterine perforation, more likely with type 2 fibroids
  • Incomplete resection requiring a second procedure
  • Intrauterine adhesion formation postop
Pimping-ready facts
  • FIGO 0/1 usually resectable in one setting; type 2 often needs staging
  • Home med holds (e.g., GLP-1 agonists) apply regardless of how "minor" the procedure is
  • Bipolar resectoscopes use saline (safer, higher fluid deficit threshold) vs. monopolar (needs electrolyte-poor media)
Your role — what to do
  • Confirm Mounjaro/GLP-1 hold was completed per protocol
  • In the OR: track fluid deficit running total — ask if not posted visibly
  • Know FIGO type of this specific fibroid before the case if documented on imaging
🟣 AUB, Thickened EMS → HSC D&C, IUD
Anatomy
  • Endometrial stripe = both endometrial layers measured together on TVUS
  • Normal: varies by cycle phase premenopausally; generally <4mm reassuring if postmenopausal + non-bleeding
  • Thickened stripe = increased glandular proliferation — could be benign hyperplasia or premalignant/malignant
Indications
  • AUB with thickened stripe on imaging, esp. perimenopausal/postmenopausal
  • Risk factors for unopposed estrogen: obesity, PCOS, nulliparity, tamoxifen
Medical management
  • Benign hyperplasia without atypia → progestin therapy (oral or IUD) first-line, avoids hysterectomy
  • Atypical hyperplasia or cancer found → management escalates (often hysterectomy)
Surgical steps

D&C/sampling first (send pathology) → if benign, levonorgestrel IUD placed → confirm fundal placement.

Blood supply — what to consider
  • The endometrium is supplied by spiral arterioles (functional layer, shed with menses) and basal arterioles (basal layer, not shed) — both branch from the radial arteries within the myometrium
  • Curettage scrapes the functional layer down to the basalis — some bleeding from disrupted spiral arterioles is expected and typically self-limited
  • No named major vessel is at risk — this is the lowest blood-supply-risk case on your list; hemostasis is usually not a significant intraoperative concern
  • IUD placement itself carries no vascular risk beyond the small risk of perforation
Complications
  • Uterine perforation during D&C or IUD insertion
  • IUD malposition/expulsion
  • Missed focal lesion — blind D&C samples ~60% of cavity vs. hysteroscopy-guided
Pimping-ready facts
  • Unopposed estrogen = core driver of hyperplasia (PCOS/anovulation, obesity via peripheral aromatization, tamoxifen's estrogenic endometrial effect)
  • Levonorgestrel IUD thins endometrium locally — first-line for hyperplasia without atypia
  • Sampling always precedes definitive treatment — never treat empirically without tissue diagnosis here
Your role — what to do
  • Confirm Mirena was ordered/available (already tracked on your list — double check it's in the room)
  • Know the order of operations: sample → confirm benign → then IUD, and be ready to explain why the order matters if asked
🟢 AUB-L/O + Endometriosis → TLH, BS, cysto ± cystectomy/endo excision/CRS
Anatomy
  • Endometriosis = endometrial-like glands/stroma outside the uterus
  • Common sites: ovaries (endometriomas), pelvic peritoneum, uterosacral ligaments, posterior cul-de-sac, bladder, bowel (rectosigmoid most common — deep infiltrating disease/DIE)
  • Chronic inflammation → dense fibrotic adhesions, can fuse organs together ("frozen pelvis")
  • Infundibulopelvic (IP) ligament — contains the ovarian artery and vein (ovarian artery direct off the aorta) plus lymphatics/nerves; this is the ligament transected for BS/oophorectomy when ovaries can't be conserved (e.g., destroyed by endometrioma). The ureter runs medial and deep to it as it crosses the pelvic brim — identify the ureter before clamping the IP ligament, especially critical here since endometriosis distorts normal planes and can directly encase the ureter.
  • Round ligament — from uterine cornu through the inguinal canal to the labia majora; contains Sampson's artery. First structure divided to open the retroperitoneum and get a clean view of the ureter and pelvic vessels before proceeding into distorted, adhesion-laden tissue.
  • Posterior cul-de-sac obliteration (common in severe disease) fuses the rectum to the posterior uterus/cervix — must be carefully dissected off before completing colpotomy
Indications
  • Severe/refractory dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, dyschezia
  • Failed medical management
  • Large/symptomatic endometriomas, suspected deep infiltrating disease (bowel/bladder)
  • Combined with AUB from fibroids/ovulatory dysfunction warranting definitive surgery
Medical management (tried first)
  • NSAIDs, combined OCPs or continuous progestin-only therapy
  • GnRH agonists/antagonists — induce hypoestrogenic state to suppress implants
  • Controls symptoms but doesn't remove disease; not fertility-preserving if pregnancy desired soon
Surgical steps — structures to identify first
  • Ureters bilaterally, from pelvic brim to bladder — before any adhesiolysis or ligament transection, since endometriosis routinely distorts their normal course
  • Rectum and rectosigmoid — especially if the posterior cul-de-sac is obliterated, to plan dissection off the posterior uterus/cervix safely
  • Extent of ovarian involvement (endometrioma size, adhesions to pelvic sidewall) — determines whether ovary can be conserved or oophorectomy is needed
  • Bladder margin if anterior disease is present
Surgical steps — sequence

Diagnostic laparoscopy first → systematically map/stage disease (ASRM staging) → identify ureters bilaterally before any dissection in distorted areas → adhesiolysis to restore normal anatomic planes (release bowel/ovary/pelvic sidewall adhesions) → ovarian cystectomy for endometriomas (strip cyst wall from healthy ovarian cortex, preserving ovary if possible) or oophorectomy via IP ligament if ovary is unsalvageable → excise/ablate peritoneal and cul-de-sac implants → if cul-de-sac is obliterated, carefully develop the rectovaginal fascia (Denonvilliers' fascia) plane to separate the rectum off the posterior vagina/cervix → transect round ligament → proceed with TLH/BS (same core steps as standard TLH, including vesicouterine fascia/bladder flap, uterine vessel ligation, cardinal/uterosacral ligament transection, colpotomy) → CRS involvement if bowel wall infiltrated (shaving superficial disease, disc excision for partial-thickness, or segmental resection for full-thickness/circumferential disease, sometimes requiring mobilization through the presacral fascia) → cystoscopy at the end.

Blood supply — what gets divided
  • Uterine artery — same as standard TLH, ligated at the internal os after ureter identification
  • Ovarian artery (IP ligament) — divided if oophorectomy needed for endometrioma destruction of the ovary; otherwise only the utero-ovarian vessel
  • Superior/middle rectal arteries — become relevant only if bowel resection (segmental) is performed; branches of the inferior mesenteric and internal iliac respectively, managed by the CRS team
  • Presacral venous plexus — a dense, valveless venous network overlying the sacrum; can bleed briskly and is hard to control with cautery if disturbed during deep posterior/rectal mobilization — one of the more feared bleeding sources in radical endo surgery
  • Endometriotic implants themselves often have abundant neovascularization (new fragile vessels feeding the lesion) — expect more oozing than usual during excision of individual implants, distinct from any single named vessel
Complications
  • Bowel injury/anastomotic leak if resection performed
  • Ureteral injury — endometriosis can encase the ureter
  • Reduced ovarian reserve from cystectomy
  • Longer op time → higher VTE, blood loss, infection risk
  • Symptom recurrence even after excision
Pimping-ready facts
  • Diagnosis is definitively surgical/histologic — imaging can suggest (esp. "ground glass" endometriomas) but not confirm
  • ASRM staging doesn't correlate well with symptom severity — stage I can be very painful, stage IV can be mild
  • Multiple consents needed since extent of disease often isn't clear until the surgeon is inside
  • Classic triad: dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, dyschezia — presentation is highly variable
Your role — what to do
  • Confirm new H&P completed and all consents (endo, CRS) are in place before the case
  • Confirm bowel prep was done and GLP-1 hold completed
  • Expect a long case — pace yourself, ask questions during natural pauses rather than mid-dissection
  • This is the case to watch retraction/exposure technique closely — distorted anatomy makes this the hardest case on your list
🔴 AUB-L → TLH, BS, cystoBleeding-driven fibroid hysterectomy
How this differs from "Fibroids → TLH"

Same procedure, different primary driver. "Fibroids" often centers on bulk/pressure symptoms ± bleeding. AUB-L means the dominant clinical problem is heavy/abnormal bleeding — usually a submucosal or cavity-distorting fibroid, or one large enough to impair uterine contractility. Anatomy, blood supply, and surgical steps are identical to the fibroid TLH card above.

Indications
  • Heavy/prolonged bleeding attributed to leiomyoma (PALM-COEIN "L")
  • Refractory to medical management (hormonal therapy, TXA)
  • Childbearing complete or hysterectomy preferred over myomectomy
Medical management tried first
  • TXA, hormonal suppression (progestin IUD, combined OCPs, GnRH agonist/antagonist)
  • Escalation to surgery when bleeding uncontrolled or anemia significant
Pimping-ready facts
  • Be ready to explain why this fibroid caused bleeding specifically (location matters: submucosal > intramural > subserosal for bleeding risk)
  • PALM-COEIN coding exists because AUB can have multiple simultaneous contributing causes (e.g., fibroids "L" + perimenopausal anovulation "O")
Your role — what to do
  • Same prep checklist as the Fibroids → TLH card (EUA, T&S/PAT decision, cystoscopy at the end)
  • Be ready to articulate the bleeding-specific reasoning if asked, even though the procedure itself is identical

See the Fibroids → TLH, BS, cysto card above for full anatomy/surgical steps/complications — they apply here identically.

OR Day Details

Before the case: Meet the assigned resident in pre-op ~6:50–7am to check in the first patient. Know the patient's chief complaint and surgical indication before you scrub in.

Consent check: Confirm the patient signed the Pelvic Examination Under Anesthesia (EUA) consent form — this is your opportunity to perform a bimanual (and speculum, if applicable) exam. Ask a resident if unsure how to check this.

In the OR: If the patient consented to a student EUA, remind the team to walk you through the bimanual/speculum exam. Otherwise, expect to assist with retraction, uterine manipulation, and closing port sites.

Uterine manipulation is genuinely difficult — you're the "5th arm." Expect frequent hand adjustments; you are not graded on how well you manipulate the uterus.

Careligns — Step by Step

Major cases (hysterectomies, urogyn, etc.) get admitted → need a Carelign. Minor cases (hysteroscopy, D&C, lap minors) go home same-day → no Carelign needed.

Do it the night before — helps you prep for the case and saves a day-of task.

  1. Find your case on the OR Status Board.
  2. Double-click the patient/surgery you're in.
  3. Go to the Carelign tab (search top bar if not starred).
  4. Click the "Summary one-liner" box.
  5. Use dot phrase .gynonc — populates template: one-liner, med hx, surg hx, OR course, labs, imaging.
  6. Fill in what you can (med hx, surg hx). Leave OR course blank until after the case. Delete the onc history section. Be succinct — use standard acronyms (HTN, PE, etc.).
  7. One-liner should be written for next-day rounds: state they are POD1 and use the next day's date.
  8. Go to Care Plan → add an Active/Acute Problem titled "Postop", and another titled "Global."
  9. Add bundles: click "+ Bundle" → "All" → search:
    • Postop → "HUP GYN ONC Post Op (MIS & Open)"
    • Global → "HUP GYN ONC Post Op Global All Patients"

Bundles auto-populate the by-system plan and pre-discharge to-do tasks for the team.

Postop AM Presentation — Structure

General outline for presenting a postop patient on benign gyn/gyn onc rounds.

  1. One-liner — as written in the Carelign. On onc, this can be cluttered — pare down to what's important. Make sure you actually understand what surgery they had and why before presenting it.
  2. Overnight events — how postop checks went, anything that happened overnight. Check with the intern before signout in case there were verbal updates from the overnight resident not yet in Carelign.
  3. Vital signs — summarize, don't list ranges: "afebrile, normotensive, satting well on room air." If abnormal, give specifics (e.g., "hypertensive to the 160s," "desatted to 89%"). Include UOP and drain output over the last 12 hours. Adequate UOP ≥ 0.5 cc/kg/hr.
  4. Labs — don't list everything. Always report the POD1 hemoglobin drop: "Starting Hgb was 11, this AM 9.3, after EBL of 200." Note other significant abnormalities only; otherwise "rest of CBC/BMP unremarkable." Ask for help interpreting this — it's genuinely hard as a student.
  5. Subjective / milestones (if you pre-rounded) — pain control, ambulating, voiding without foley, tolerating diet, passing gas.
  6. Exam — only pertinent findings based on her problems. E.g.: "Belly soft/non-tender/non-distended, midline vertical incision intact with staples, clean/dry/non-erythematous. Foley draining clear urine, JP with serosanguinous output." Tailor to relevant systems (e.g., heart/lung/LE edema if she has heart failure).
  7. Plan by system — start from Carelign but use judgment and expand: "On clear liquids, tolerating well → advance to regular diet." Or: Carelign says hold amlodipine for chronic HTN, but if POD2 and normo/hypertensive, suggest restarting.
  8. Summary / to-do's for the day — close with the plan: "To summarize: active trial of void, advance diet, PM CBC to follow up significant Hgb drop, switch to PO pain meds."
Remember: Reliability on "scut" (Careligns, discharge summaries) builds trust fast — do it well and early, without needing double-checks.
